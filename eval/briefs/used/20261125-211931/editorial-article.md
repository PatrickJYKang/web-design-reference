Title: The last ice house on the Humber

Standfirst: For a century, Grimsby froze its catch with ice made in a brick factory the size of a cathedral. The factory is still standing. Nobody has decided what it is for.

Byline: Words by Marta Lindqvist. Photographs by Joe Abara. 19-minute read.

Lead image caption: The Grimsby Ice Factory from Gorton Street, March. The tanks that held 1,200 tonnes of ice a day are still inside.

Body:

The first thing you notice is the smell, which is not fish. It is cold brick, and something mineral underneath it, like the inside of a kettle. The building has been shut since 1990 and the smell has not left.

When it opened in 1901 the factory could make more ice in a day than the town's trawlers could carry. Blocks the weight of a man slid down chutes to the quay, were crushed, and were blown through hoses into the holds of ships bound for Iceland and the White Sea. Without it there was no distant-water fleet, and without the fleet there was not much of a town.

Pull quote: "People ask me if it could be flats. I ask them if they have been inside."

Subheading: What it costs to keep a ruin

The roof alone would take four million pounds, according to the trust that has looked after the site since 2010. The compressors, which are the reason the building is listed, would take more, and nobody alive has run them.

Inline image caption: One of four J&E Hall ammonia compressors, installed 1931.

End note: Marta Lindqvist writes about ports and the towns behind them. Her last piece for Lowtide was on the Dover pilots.
